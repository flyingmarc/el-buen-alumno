## GENERAL INFORMATION & PREPARATION

Whether you are an integrated or modular student, how you prepare before coming for your lesson, sets the tone for your entire flight. Thorough preparation quietens the mind, lowers your cockpit workload in the air, and gives you the mental capacity to truly enjoy flying and absorb every lesson.

Additionally a respectful and honest post flight review (self or with your instructor) will help you identify trends and help you improve step by step all while mitigating risks and creating real achievements.

### Preparation for Every Flight

Starting from lesson 1, prepare for the briefing with a complete, fully updated flight folder. Name the folder following the standard date format: DD-MM-YY (e.g., 01-12-26 for a flight on December 1st, 2026).

Your folder should contain the following core operational documents:

| Document | Name in File | Description | Primary Source |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Mass and Balance | MB | Accurate calculation of Ramp Weight, Take-off Weight, and Landing Weight against Center of Gravity (CG) envelope limits. Must be verified and signed for the current flight date. | Private Radar |
| Significant Weather Chart | SIG06/SIG12/SIG18 | Synoptic weather charts displaying frontal activity, turbulence, icing layers, and cloud coverage across the operating area. | AEMET/AMA |
| NOTAMS | NOTAM | Notices to Airmen covering departure, destination, alternate aerodromes, and enroute airspace (temporary danger areas, navaid outages, unserviceable lighting). | ICARO Enaire |

### The Airmanship Hierarchy: Aviate, Navigate, Communicate

Whenever workload spikes or an unexpected challenge arises, ground yourself in the time-tested pilot hierarchy:

1. **Aviate (Primary Priority):** Maintain control of the aircraft at all times. Pitch attitude, bank angle, balance, and trim take absolute precedence over maps, radio calls, and checklists. Never let an administrative cockpit task compromise aircraft handling. Fly the plane.
2. **Navigate:** Monitor spatial orientation, assigned headings, planned track, wind drift, and clearance above obstacles and safe terrain altitudes.
3. **Communicate & Systems Management:** Execute standard checklist flows, conduct required ATC transmissions, manage fuel selectors, and monitor engine parameters once the aeroplane is established on path.
4. **Automate and Delegate:** Use your Equipment (eg. Autopilot) and others (Passengers, another Pilot, Instructor) if possible and available to reduce your workload.

It is completely normal during the first phase of training to constantly feel behind the Aircraft. We start out training, reacting to changes and situations that have already happened or are currently happening, but slowly through the training you will transform to staying ahead of the aircraft reacting to changes as they happen or even before they happen. To help this process use quiet, low-workload cruising phases (such as established level cruise) to prepare for what comes next.

Complete cruise checks (e.g., FREDAS flow), carburetor icing checks, approach reviews, and landing briefings early so your workload remains calm and deliberate when arriving at the circuit.

### FREDAS Checks

**F - Fuel**
*   Fuel selector: Set to the proper tank (execute tank change if required).
*   Parameters: Check fuel quantity and pressure, and ensure the fuel pump is set as required.

**R - Radio & Navigation**
*   Primary Comms: Active frequency in contact; standby tuned to your next sector frequency.
*   Secondary Comms: Set to ATIS or emergency frequencies.
*   Transponder: Set to the correct code and active mode.

**E - Engine & Systems**
*   Gauges: All engine temperatures and pressures operating in the green range.
*   Power: Power set for cruise, mixture leaned as required.
*   Ice: Check for carburetor ice and apply carb heat if necessary.

**D - Directional Gyro (DI)**
*   Alignment: Verify the DI is synchronized with the magnetic compass and confirm your current heading.

**A - Altitude, Altimeter & Airspace**
*   Altimeter: Ensure the correct pressure setting (QNH or Standard) is set.
*   Airspace: Maintain safe altitude as required, ensure you are clear of any active NOTAM areas, and anticipate your next climb/descent profiles and waypoints.

**S - Speed & Suction**
*   Speed: Indicated airspeed verified; adjust power as required.
*   Suction (Added): Verify the suction gauge is operating in the green range.

*Note: FREDAS Checks are done slightly differently sometimes due to workload and session objective - the Big picture is what counts - Check your Aircraft regularly to catch any abnormal behavior as soon as possible.*

> **Did you know?** Preparation, mentally and physically on ground will be your best tool to get ahead of the aircraft. By practicing radio calls on ground you will develop a format and flow which will be easier to recall in the air. By practicing briefings and checklist flows, on ground or air home, you will notice abnormal situations quicker and be able to detect skipped or missed steps easier. And again when in the air or in a stressful situation it will be easier to recall parts of the flows and connect them with the checklists.

### How to ChairFly and Brief

Visualizing, or chair flying has been used by aviators around the world since forever and are actively used by military and air show pilots around the world. You can practice by just sitting in the aircraft and actually touching the controls (no need to switch anything on or off) and talking out the actions while confirming with the checklist. By muscle memory you will retain much of the information and be faster and more accurately at recalling it in high stress situations.

If you do not like to do that, you can simply take a picture of the cockpit and look at it at your phone or iPad and imagine step by step how you would look for and manipulate the controls and switches. The simple fact you have looked and identified the controls before will help you find them easier and faster.

The easiest and quickest but still very effective way is to just close your eyes and imagine the cockpit while going through the flows. This is actually best in conjuction done with an image of the cockpit in the begining to double check you know the location of controls before eventually fully imagining them.

The simple fact you are looking for and manipulating controls in a particular order (called a flow) will manifest in you and help you become more confident, quicker and accurate in your actions. I would recommend you to complete a full flight from startup to shutdown, following checklists as well as radio calls at least once before your lessons until your solo flight. After that as much as. You want or need, but at least on every new aircraft type you fly or new exercises you plan to do.

Briefing yourself like as if you have a co-pilot, further helps to cement your own understanding of maneuvers and actions and even maybe question yourself to improve your flows to become more efficient and direct.

**A Briefing should include:**
*   a clear achievement or goal
*   clear steps and metrics to follow step by step
*   easy to follow and a clear understandable order
*   contingency planning (what if something goes wrong)
*   should be adopted to the current situation

The best example for this would be a take off briefing. By briefing yourself you have "lived" though the scenario at hand already and covered the most common points. Briefings are mandatory in many airlines and will be a big part of IFR flying so the earlier you start to include them in your flights the better.

### Your Instructor

Love them or hate them, your instructors are here to help you—and they are your absolute best resource when things get challenging. Throughout your training, and eventually your career, you will fly with a variety of instructors and captains. Each will have a slightly different way of teaching and presenting information. Naturally, you will work better with some than with others, but the core principle remains: they have the knowledge and experience you need. Adapting to different people and figuring out how to extract that knowledge is a critical career skill for any professional pilot.

**Working Together Effectively**
*   **Be Professional:** Arrive polite, respectful, prepared, and interested. If you prepare thoroughly for your lessons on the ground, your instructor can spend less time on basic explanations and more time actually refining your flying technique.
*   **Communicate Clearly:** Ask for clarification the moment you don't understand something, and take critique constructively. Instructors do not expect perfection, but they do look for a willingness to learn and adapt.
*   **Develop the PIC Mindset:** While the instructor is there to guide you, part of your training is learning how to act as the Pilot in Command. If you feel overwhelmed, need a moment to catch up to the aircraft, or feel a situation is pushing beyond your current limits, speak up. A good pilot knows when to say "Standby" or hand over controls.
*   **Manage Conflicts:** If you feel unfairly treated or frustrated, try to clear the air with your instructor calmly. Tell them directly how you feel and why. If you absolutely cannot resolve a conflict, you can escalate the issue to the Head of Training (HT) or Chief Flight Instructor (CFI). However, remember that how you handle disagreements reflects your ability to work within a multi-crew environment. Sometimes, taking a step back to reflect on the flight is the best first step.

These principles apply equally to ground staff, refuelers, ramp agents, and everyone else you will interact with. Aviation is an incredibly small world; there is a very high chance you will cross paths with these individuals again down the line. Keep your relationships positive or at the very least neutral. You have an amazing team available to you at the academy, so be sure to use them!

### Scheduling and Cancelations

Like your Instructor, Scheduling or Asset Planning is another department you will have mixed feelings about. Scheduling is incredibly complex and dynamic. There is constant changes to the available Aircraft / Students / Instructors / Examiners / Simulators - and Scheduling has to keep up on top of that with limitations of duty times, potentials and delays. In short the schedule is always subject to changes. I would recommend to check your schedule every evening (after 6-7pm) and before getting ready for your flight.

Also do check the weather (including temperature and winds) for your expected departure and arrival time. Your instructor will make the decision to fly or cancel the lesson, but keep in mind that instructors might not be at the airfield much earlier than you, so there will be times where you arrive at the field just to have the mission cancelled. This is especially true for the first flights in the morning. This is unfortunately part of aviation and will happen in commercial airlines and any other flying jobs you might do.

If you arrive from Valencia or any further places you can check with Operations for any updates on your Session but keep in mind, the Supervisor does not cancel your session, he/she can check with your Instructor and give you an idea how the chances are for you to fly, but that's about it. Avoid contacting them for every flight.

For the first few flights and at certain stages, your instructor will do a briefing with you regardless of the weather - use the time to ask questions and get clarifications. At some stages there is also the possibility to change to a Sim session instead of the flight.

Why are some flights cancelled while others are not? Instructors will decide to fly or not depending on a number of factors, apart from weather it depends on the students individual abilities and the lesson objective and stage the student is in.

### Performance Factoring Principles

In your POH you will find tables to calculate various performance figures. At the very least a Pilot should know how much runway is needed to take off and land, because that is after all what you are going to do. Since LERE runway is situated in a micro climate of its own and has some unique properties, spend some time figuring out what weather conditions have what effect on your performance.

Performance charts in your aircraft Flight Manual (POH) represent new aircraft flown by factory test pilots under ideal conditions. In real operations, regulatory safety factors are applied to provide a reassuring safety cushion against runway slope, surface variations, engine age, and human reaction time:
*   **Factored Take-off Distance:** Regulatory safety factors ensure the aircraft can comfortably clear all screen heights or reject the takeoff with runway to spare.
*   **Factored Landing Distance:** Factoring landing distances ensures you always have margin to spare even if the flare floats slightly or braking friction is less than optimal.
*   **Intersection Departures:** Never assume you have the full physical runway length available when joining from an intermediate taxiway. Always calculate performance based strictly on the declared distances (TORA, TODA, ASDA) published in the official AIP charts for that specific intersection. As a student it is perfectly acceptable to add your personal safety margin to this.

**Deep Dive: Regulatory Performance Formulas (EU-OPS / Part-NCO)**
For students wishing to calculate exact performance numbers and verify safety margins:

1.  **Take-Off Factoring:**
    *   Without Clearway / Stopway: Factored TODR = Gross $TODR\times1.25\le TORA$
    *   With Clearway and/or Stopway: Gross $TODR\le TORA$, Gross $TODR\times1.15\le TODA$, Gross $TODR\times1.30\le ASDA$
2.  **Landing Factoring (Dry Runway - EU OPS 1.550):**
    *   Factored LDR = Gross LDR $\times 1.43 \le LDA$
    *   *Note:* Requiring full stop landing within 70% of available runway length $(LDA\times0.70\ge \text{Gross LDR})$ is mathematically identical to multiplying Gross LDR by 1.43 $(1/0.70\approx1.43)$
3.  **Environmental & Surface Corrections (if not built into AFM charts):**
    *   Runway Downslope (Landing): Increase LDR by 5% for every 1% downslope (up to 2% maximum).
    *   Grass Runway (Dry): Multiply distance by 1.15 (+15%).
    *   Wet Runway (Paved or Grass): Multiply distance by 1.15 (+15%).
4.  **Pressure Altitude (PA) Calculation:**
    *   $PA = \text{Aerodrome Elevation} (ft) + (1013-QNH) \times 27 ft/hPa$
    *   *Example:* Elevation = 92ft, QNH = 1020 hPa $\rightarrow (1013-1020) \times 27 = -189 ft \rightarrow PA = 92 - 189 =$ -97 ft.

When planning routes to Airports other than LERE, take into account that you might be asked to take off from an intersection, it is your responsiblility to know if you are able to do so. Do check the AIP for lengths and rules applicable to intersection takeoffs. Once again the better you are prepared, the more options you will have available to you and the better your chances of success. Would you accept an interception take off when instructed form ATC without knowing if you have enough runway available to take off?

### Conceptual Fuel Planning

Fuel planning is about managing energy reserves safely from engine start to final shutdown:
*   **Taxi & Run-up Allowance:** Ground operations consume fuel before takeoff. Always budget for startup and taxiing.
*   **Climb Fuel Increment:** High climb power burns fuel at a noticeably higher rate than cruising flight.
*   **En-route & Circuit Fuel:** Calculated using the planned power settings and true airspeeds from your aircraft POH. When in doubt, calculate with a higher power setting than planned.
*   **Contingency & Alternates:** Fuel set aside for weather deviations, holding, or routing to your alternate aerodrome.
*   **Final Reserve:** The legal minimum fuel that must remain untouched in the tanks upon shutdown.

Develop a system to remind yourself of fuel tank switches and make note of the times, especially when flying solo. Never trust the fuel gauges. Bring a watch and set a timer.

## Part 1: Basic Phase & General Control

This phase builds the foundation of your flying skills. Modular students start here, while integrated students will use this as their core building block.

### What Is Expected
Understand the four aerodynamic forces acting on the aircraft during straight and level flight, climbs, descents, and turns. These forces are lift, weight, thrust, and drag. In unaccelerated, steady flight, these opposing forces are in a state of equilibrium, meaning lift equals weight and thrust equals drag. You should be able to fly to visual reference points, make standard radio calls, conduct structured pre-takeoff briefings, and handle everyday normal and some abnormal scenarios.

### The Four Forces
Lift acts upward perpendicular to the relative wind through the center of lift, directly opposing weight, which pulls vertically downward through the aircraft's center of gravity.
Thrust, generated by the powerplant, provides the forward force to overcome drag.

*   Thrust
*   Lift
*   Weight
*   Drag

Visual flying is centered outside: the relationship between the aircraft cowling and the horizon is your primary attitude reference. Straight and level flight is achieved by referencing the aircraft's nose directly in front of you against the natural horizon—the line where the sky meets the sea or terrain. Take a moment on your prep flight to adjust your seat and establish a good seating position.

You establish the correct pitch attitude (for instance, setting the nose around three to four fingers below the horizon) with the elevator, maintain laterally level wings with the ailerons, and keep the aircraft in balance using the rudder.

You will learn how to trim the aircraft effectively to fly with finger-tip precision, utilizing the core sequence of Power, Attitude, Trim (PAT). Effective trimming follows a specific methodology to achieve a stable, hands-off state:
*   **Power:** Set the power for your selected flight performance.
*   **Attitude:** Use the control stick to select and hold the desired pitch and bank attitude.
*   **Trim:** Use the trim control in the natural direction—making coarse adjustments first, then fine adjustments to relieve the physical pressure on the stick.
*   **Change, Check, Hold:** When making flight path corrections, proactively change the attitude, check the reference point or instruments, hold the new attitude, and re-trim.

By consistently neutralizing control loads through precise trimming, your hands can come off the controls while the aircraft remains perfectly level.

*Why do we want to fly with the hands off the controls?* Your instructor might tell you its because we pilots are lazy but in reality its because in your next phases you will need your hands to hold maps and charts, calculate diversions and corrections and note down clearances or setup instruments. It is true as well that it makes for a much more relaxed flight if you can trim the aircraft without constant control inputs, this is especially handy in the traffic pattern.

### Critical Maneuvers
You must know what happens at the stall and how to recover from both incipient and full stalls. You will practice slow flight and steep turns, developing balanced, coordinated control inputs while climbing, descending, or turning. Your best friend should again become the trim.

### The Stall
The fundamental foundation of upset prevention begins with unlearning a dangerous cognitive shortcut: an airplane does not stall because it is flying too slowly; it stalls because it exceeds the Critical Angle of Attack. We associate slow speed with the stall as flying at low speeds without losing altitude usually results in a high angle of attack, it is however possible to exceed the critical angle of Attack at high speeds as well as turns, climbs or descends. To break the stall or recover from the stall your first reaction needs to be to lower the Angle of Attack. Power is added to avoid excessive altitude loss and wings are kept level to avoid getting in a Spin, or if already in a Spin to help recovery.

| Recovery Stage | Nose-High Upset (Low Airspeed) | Nose-Low Upset (High Airspeed) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Pitch | Push forward to lower the nose and prevent a stall | Release back pressure before rolling, then pull up smoothly once level |
| Roll | Use bank to let the nose drop naturally to the horizon | Roll wings level via the shortest route |
| Thrust | Reduce power if the engine is forcing the nose up | Throttle to idle to stop speeding up; use if available |
| Secondary Risk | Stalling, spinning, or sliding backwards | Overstressing the wings (too many Gs) or overspeeding drag |

### Systems Knowledge & Checklist Disciplines
Understand basic aircraft systems, including fuel, electrical, engine, and propeller mechanics. Learn the two ways to use checklists:
*   **Flow and Check:** For active, time-critical phases (takeoff line-up, downwind, landing, critical emergencies), memorize a logical physical flow across the panel, then confirm critical items using the written checklist.
*   **Read and Do:** For stationary or non-time-critical procedures (pre-flight checks, engine start) and non-critical emergency checklists, read each step and complete it methodically.

### Basic Instruments & Cockpit Layout Fundamentals
Understanding your cockpit layout and basic instruments reduces cognitive load. Familiarize yourself with these core systems:
*   **Fuel System:** Know your tank capacities, usable vs. unusable fuel, fuel selectors (LEFT, RIGHT, BOTH, OFF), and the importance of checking for water/contaminants during preflight.
*   **Engine & Brakes:** Monitor oil pressure/temperature and cylinder head temperatures. Verify brake pedal pressure and toe-brake operation during taxi.
*   **Pitot-Static System:** This system drives your Airspeed Indicator, Altimeter, and Vertical Speed Indicator (VSI). The pitot tube measures ram air pressure, while the static port measures ambient pressure. Ensure both are clear of blockages (like bugs or ice) during preflight.
*   **Vacuum System:** Powers gyroscopic instruments like the Attitude Indicator and Heading Indicator. Always check the vacuum gauge (suction in the green arc) after engine start or at least during runup to ensure gyros are spooling up correctly.

### Do it like an Airline Pilot
It is common practice in airlines to minimize conversations to essential topics during take off, landing and taxiing, these are called Sterile Cockpit Phases. While taxiing you should concentrate on that task without adjusting or setting up instruments, refrain from reading checklists or charts during taxi. If you absolutely need to do something either stop or slow down the aircraft, if it is save to do so, or ask to hand over controls to your instructor.

When approaching or leaving an airfield from or to one of the Entry or Exit points (NESW) it is good practice to focus on listing for other traffic, ATIS and keep conversations to a minimum. Things like: briefings, the landing or take off checks and operational questions or prompts. During cruise you can discuss any events that happened or clarify questions etc..

## Part 2: Solo Flight, Personal Limits & Circuit Mastery

Stepping into the circuit as a solo pilot is one of the proudest moments in any aviator's life. It marks the milestone where you transition from being guided by an instructor to acting as the true Pilot-in-Command. At our academy, this transition is built on a foundation of mutual trust, deep respect, and rigorous preparation.

During the Maneuver sessions, start looking at your course syllabus in Private Radar and maybe discuss the next sessions ahead of time with your instructors to prepare for your Solo release. There is very limited time scheduled for practicing the Traffic Pattern and landings, so its best to start early in your course. It is very common for students to ask for extra practice or for instructors to discuss the possibilities of repeating a session to get some more practice or confidence. Follow and feel the controls during landings and take offs as soon as you feel comfortable. Always check with your instructor and consider the possibilities to take over limited or full control during take offs or landings.

The actual solo flight is a combination off two lessons sheduled right behind each other. First you will do a 90 minute session with an instructor where he or she will act as a safety pilot only (=not touching the controls unless safety related) and will review some maneuvers, emergencies and landings in different configurations. Then immediately after this session, if you are ready to go Solo, the instructor will leave the aircraft and give you a sign over the Radio to continue on your own. Your instructor will be "with you" over the radio and keep an eye on you, while you fly the plane on your own in the traffic pattern performing Touch and goes.

### Cockpit Communication & The Learning Mindset
*   **Positive Handover of Controls:** Ambiguity about who is flying is dangerous. Always execute a clear, positive three-way exchange: "You have control" $\rightarrow$ "I have control" $\rightarrow$ "You have control". When your instructor asks you to "follow through," rest your hands and feet lightly on the controls to feel the subtle pressures and visual cues without fighting the movement. If at any point the Instructor announces "My Controls / I have control" relieve the controls, including the rudder or brakes. If the situation permits the FI will always try to let you follow through on the controls.
*   **Navigating the Circuit Plateau:** Almost every student encounters a temporary learning plateau during circuit consolidation— where progress seems to level off and landings feel unpredictable. Understand that this is a completely normal psychological phase where your brain is assimilating complex visual and motor skills. Be patient with yourself. Focus on the basics you can control: chair-flying the pattern at home, visualizing sight pictures, and speaking radio calls, checklists and briefings aloud.
*   **Information Capacity & Task Saturation:** Human working memory comfortably handles only $7\pm2$ items at any given moment. When you feel rushed in the circuit, task saturation sets in. You solve this not by rushing your hands, but by establishing structured flows and staying well ahead of the aircraft. Similar to a recovery from a stall, no need to rush but follow deliberate purposeful flows.
*   **Pushing the limits vs building confidence and comfort:** During various phases of your training you might encounter days and flights where the weather, airport operations or the interactions with the instructor feel more challenging than what you have experienced up to this point. You might be tempted to try out the limits and push your abilities which is a good way to gain more experience and build strength and resilience. However do consider as well that flying in these conditions can act like a reset or break of your confidence and reset your progress a bit. Flying in adverse weather conditions or an extremely busy day at the airport, for example, can be a great experience and further sharpen your skills but it can also be a horrible experience, a failed lesson objective and a complete damper to your confidence. Your best resource is going to be a thorough briefing with your Instructor, weighting the pros and cons and a informed, grounded decision "to go" or "to not go".

### Visual Lookout & Human Vision Realities
*   **Sector Scanning:** The central visual field struggles to detect small, stationary objects (such as an aircraft on a converging collision course) due to empty-sky myopia, whereas peripheral vision detects relative movement instantly. Scan outside in systematic $20^{\circ}$ sectors. Move your head and upper body to look around cabin pillars and wing roots before every turn.
*   **Confirmation Bias:** When identifying landmarks or runway thresholds, pilots often look for what they expect to see rather than what is actually there. Cross-check multiple visual features before confirming a reference point.
*   Spotting other Traffic even in the pattern is a skill. Listening to position reports and developing a mental map of other traffic, is the start to spotting other traffic. It is easiest to see traffic that is turning, so look for areas where you expect traffic to change from upwind to crosswind, crosswind to downwind etc..

### EASA KSA Philosophy for Solo Flight
The EASA Knowledge, Skills, and Attitudes (KSA) philosophy ensures that you develop not only the physical stick-and-rudder abilities but also the mindset of a professional pilot. KSA evaluates your decision-making, threat and error management, and how you gather information to set safe limits. Your instructor will look for maturity, risk awareness, and self-discipline before signing your solo endorsement. Being able to land an Aircraft is not the only skill required to be released for your Solo.

### Flight Risk Assessment Tool (FRAT)
Before flights especially Solo flights, you can judge your level of risk by completing a FRAT to quantify your personal limits. A FRAT objectively scores factors such as:
*   **Pilot:** Rest, stress, currency, and recent flying hours.
*   **Aircraft:** Known squawks, equipment status, and performance margins.
*   **Environment:** Crosswind components, cloud bases, visibility, and density altitude.
*   **External Pressures:** Self-imposed rush, passenger expectations, or schedule constraints.

If the FRAT score exceeds your personal or academy limits, you must delay, cancel, or take an instructor with you.

## 2.1 The Traffic Pattern (Circuit Training)

The aerodrome traffic circuit is an orderly, standardized pattern designed to ensure safe separation between arriving and departing traffic while establishing a stabilized approach to touchdown.

**Core Objectives:**
1. Fly a standardized, balanced traffic circuit conforming to aerodrome procedures and right-of-way rules, completing safe landings within tolerances.
2. Safely and instinctively manage an Engine Failure After Takeoff (EFATO) or balked landing with calm, prioritized control.

### Circuit Threat & Error Management (Τ.Ε.Μ.) Matrix

| Operational Threat | Undesired Aircraft State | Pilot Countermeasure |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Engine Failure After Takeoff (EFATO) | Forced landing / aerodynamic stall | Brief departure emergencies before rolling. Lower the nose immediately to best glide attitude; land straight ahead within $XX^{\circ}$ of runway heading dependent on altitude. Never attempt to turn back at low level. |
| Circuit Traffic Convergence | Loss of separation / conflict | Maintain visual scanning and active radio watch; promptly follow ATC spacing instructions (orbits or holding procedures). Leave the pattern if it becomes too congested. Ask for clarifications if unsure of other traffic positions. Make clear and precise calls and adjustments to separate from other traffic. |
| Unstable Final Approach | Long flare, heavy touchdown, loss of control | Enforce stabilized approach criteria (configured, on speed, on path). Initiate an immediate go-around if parameters drift. |
| Omitted Pre-Landing Checks | Altimeter setting error / wrong configuration / incorrect runway selection | Follow structured flow patterns; back up with physical written checklist; cross-check airfield QNH against aerodrome elevation. Check windsock or wind drift. |

### Principles of Flight in the Pattern
*   **Slipstream & Left-Turning Tendency:** At high engine power and low airspeed (takeoff and climb), propeller slipstream strikes the left side of the vertical fin, yawing the nose to the left. Proactively apply smooth, positive right rudder to track the centerline accurately.
*   **Wind & Climb Angles:** Taking off into the wind reduces ground roll and steepens your climb angle relative to the ground, providing superior obstacle clearance per meter of forward travel.
*   **Drift Compensation:** Wind rarely blows directly down the runway. Compensate for drift on every leg of the circuit to maintain a square, rectangular track over the ground. Keep wings level and crab into the wind so your ground track remains parallel or perpendicular to the runway.

### Visual Geometry: Lateral Spacing & Aiming Points
*   **Too Close to Runway:** The runway appears excessively tall and wide. This compresses your timeline, forcing steep bank angles on base-to-final and leaving zero room to stabilize speed and descent rate.
*   **Too Wide from Runway:** The runway appears as a flat, narrow strip. You spend prolonged time at low altitude, struggle to judge glide angles, and risk conflict with outside traffic.
*   **Balanced Lateral Spacing:** Position the aircraft so the runway sits in a natural, moderate trapezoidal perspective (e.g., positioned across your airframe reference point, such as halfway down the wing strut on high-wing aircraft).
*   **Aiming Point Cues:** Pick a distinct point on the runway (such as the threshold numbers). If that aiming point moves up in your windscreen, you are going below glidepath; if it moves down, you are going high. Adjust power to control glidepath, and adjust pitch to hold your approach speed.

### Step-by-Step Circuit Workflow
Refer to your aircraft POH and SOP for exact speeds, power settings, and flap selections.

1.  **Takeoff Roll:** Line up on the centerline, rolling forward a few meters to ensure the nosewheel is straight. Pick a visual alignment point on the far end of the runway, and a higher reference beyond the aerodrome for climb-out. Apply smooth takeoff power, maintain centerline with rudder, confirm engine gauges in the green and airspeed active, and rotate smoothly at published rotation speed.
2.  **Climb Out / Upwind:** Maintain climb attitude and track the extended centerline with wings level. At 400 ft AGL, clear the airspace left and right, then initiate a balanced turn onto crosswind.
3.  **Crosswind Leg:** Continue climbing to circuit altitude. Establish a crab angle into wind to track perpendicular to the runway. Anticipate leveling off slightly before reaching circuit altitude.
4.  **Downwind Leg:** Level off smoothly using the sequence: Pitch for level horizon, Power set to circuit cruise, and Trim to relieve all control loads. Complete downwind pre-landing checks (e.g., BUMFHL flow: Brakes, Undercarriage, Mixture, Fuel, Harnesses, Lights & Instruments). Abeam the landing threshold, make your standard radio call (e.g., Touch-and-Go or Full Stop).
5.  **Base Turn & Descent:** When the threshold is roughly $45^{\circ}$ behind your wing (adjusted earlier in strong headwinds, later in tailwinds), begin descent setup: reduce power, select intermediate approach flaps per SOP, and trim for intermediate approach speed. Begin a gentle descending turn onto base leg, accounting for crosswind drift. Remember: Pitch controls airspeed; power controls the rate of descent. Trim if on parameters, make your radio call.
6.  **Final Approach:** Turn smoothly onto final, aligning accurately with the centerline. Select final landing flaps and establish final approach speed and trim per your POH. Lock your visual scan onto your aiming point, adjusting power to hold the glidepath. Notify Final, check windsock and runway is clear.
7.  **Flare & Touchdown:** Over the threshold or within gliding distance - with landing assured, smoothly! retard the throttle to idle. Shift visual focus towards the far end of the runway to judge descend rate. Progressively! raise the nose attitude to hold the aircraft off the ground, allowing the main wheels to touch down gently first. Lower the nosewheel smoothly and maintain centerline with rudder.

### Crosswind Circuit & Landing Techniques
*   **Ground Handling:** Aircraft naturally weathercock into wind. Deflect ailerons into wind on the taxi roll and hold elevator neutral or forward/aft depending on wind direction to prevent gusts from lifting a wing or tail.
*   **Crab (Kick-Straight) Method:** Fly final approach in balance, crabbed into the crosswind along the centerline. Just before touchdown in the flare, smoothly apply rudder to align the longitudinal axis with the runway while using into-wind aileron to keep wings level.
*   **Wing-Down (Sideslip) Method:** Lower the upwind wing with aileron to arrest drift while using opposite rudder to align the fuselage with the centerline. The upwind main wheel touches down first, followed by the downwind wheel, then the nosewheel.
*   **The Blended Approach:** Crab down final for passenger comfort and balance, transitioning smoothly into the wing-down technique during the flare to ensure zero side-load on the landing gear.

### Abnormal Procedures: EFATO & Go-Around
*   **EFATO Golden Rule:** Never attempt to turn back to the runway at low altitude. Lower the nose immediately to your POH best glide attitude. Land straight ahead within $XX^{\circ}$ of runway heading, into wind. If height and time permit, perform the F-M-I trouble flow (Fuel pump ON/change tanks, Mixture RICH, Ignition BOTH/checked). If it does not restart, complete shutdown to minimize fire risk and fly the aircraft all the way through touchdown.
*   **Go-Around Flow (Normal Procedure, Not an Emergency):**
    1. Smoothly apply full take-off power and select carburetor heat COLD.
    2. Apply assertive right rudder to arrest slipstream yaw.
    3. Pitch up to select your climb attitude.
    4. Cross-check altimeter and VSI to verify a positive rate of climb.
    5. Retract flaps to stage 1 or Take off as per your POH (never all at once).
    6. Climb along the centerline, report go-around, and rejoin the pattern at circuit altitude.

### Joining the Circuit & Right of Way
*   **Standard Overhead Join:** When approaching an uncontrolled field, announce intentions 5-10 NM out or before reaching entry / exit points. Unless otherwise published, arrive overhead well above circuit altitude, identify the active runway and windsock, position on the non-traffic side, and descend in a shallow powered turn to cross the upwind threshold at circuit altitude to join downwind. This technique is used at LETL for example.
*   **Right of Way:** Aircraft in distress > Gliders > Landing aircraft over departing aircraft > Aircraft established in circuit over joining aircraft. Always remember: Having the right of way never excuses you from the responsibility to see, communicate, and avoid.
*   **Systems Management (SADIE Checks):** Periodically run your panel check: Suction in green, Ammeter charging, Directional Gyro aligned with compass, Icing/Carb heat checked, Engine temperatures and pressures in the green.

### **Advanced Upset Prevention & Recovery Training (UPRT)**

For some, the Advanced Upset Prevention and Recovery Training (UPRT) module is the most anticipated part of their flight training. For others, it is a significant source of anxiety. Both reactions are perfectly normal. It is important to understand from the outset: the goal of this course is not to turn you into an aerobatic pilot. The objective is to expand your situational awareness and confidence so that if you ever encounter an unintended extreme flight attitude, your reaction will be trained, calm, and correct. Additionally UPRT trains you to overcome the startle effect and surprise during unusual attitudes. 

<img width="419" height="415" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/b1dc1ad7-b187-4d83-b47e-3e62fff99dde" />

The Advanced UPRT course consists of theory followed by two dedicated flight sessions (roughly 1.5 hours each). We conduct these flights directly over the aerodrome at a safe altitude of 5,500 to 6,000 feet.

Aircraft Preparation and Configuration
Because we will be subjecting the aircraft to increased load factors, we operate strictly within the Utility Category. Make sure you use the Utility Mass and Balance sheets for these flights. You will fly these missions very light, with minimal fuel, typically around 10 gallons per tank. Additionally, ensure the engine oil is at more than 7 quarts; this helps prevent the oil pump from drawing air during zero-G or negative-G maneuvers.

Pre-flight critical check: The cabin must be completely sterile. Absolutely no loose items, pens, kneeboards, or water bottles can be left unsecured. In a zero-G environment, a loose object can jam flight controls.

Physiology and Communication
Flying unusual attitudes can induce motion sickness. We expect this, and we manage it actively.
Signs that your body is reaching its limit include breaking into a cold sweat, turning pale, or sudden changes in your overall feeling. Its common to use a  1-to-10 scale to communicate how you are feeling.
 
 * Level 1-2: You are feeling fine and ready for the next maneuver.
 * Level 3-4: You need a pause of the exercises, open the air vents, and fly straight and level for a few minutes to let your vestibular system reset.
 * Level 5-7: We need an extended break to breathe and calm down and switch to less intense exercises.  
 * Level 8-9: We stop the session and immediately turn back to land.
Never try to "tough it out" in silence. Open communication ensures you actually learn from the session rather than just enduring it.

Why Upsets Happen
An airplane rarely enters an upset on its own. Understanding the root causes is the first step in prevention:
 * Environmental Factors: Wake turbulence from heavier aircraft, severe wind shear, or mountain wave rotors can aggressively roll or pitch a light aircraft beyond normal limits.
 * Pilot-Induced / Distraction: Spatial disorientation (especially when inadvertently entering Instrument Meteorological Conditions), dropping a pen and reaching for it, or fixating on a malfunctioning instrument can cause a pilot to unconsciously input fatal control deflections.
 * System Failures: Instrument failures (like a vacuum pump failure causing a slowly tumbling attitude indicator) can trick a pilot into entering a "graveyard spiral" if they follow the faulty instrument.

The Flight Sessions
The flights focus on recognizing and recovering from Nose-High, Nose-Low, and Spiral Dive unusual attitudes. We then progresses to advanced Stall events (including accelerated and zero-G stalls), Spin recognition and Spin recovery.

Execution and Transfer of Controls
During the exercises, there is limited time for the standard "I have control / You have control" handover. The instructor will induce the upset, and at the critical moment ask you to: "Recover." That is the key for you to immediately take the flight controls and execute the correct recovery sequence.

General Recovery Principles
While every situation is unique, the core tenets of upset recovery remain consistent.
 * Nose High = Good (Manage Energy). You have altitude but are losing airspeed. You may need to add power, apply forward pressure (potentially using 0-G or gently pushing to 1-G to help the nose track down), and roll wings level only after the angle of attack is safely reduced. A common mistake here is pushing the nose down too aggressively (past 10 degrees nose down), which merely transitions a nose-high upset into a nose-low upset.
 * Nose Down = Bad (Manage Speed). You are rapidly losing altitude and gaining airspeed. The immediate priority is to reduce the throttle to idle to stop the acceleration, roll the wings level using ailerons, and carefully pull out of the dive. A crucial hazard here is pulling back too hard while at low speeds or while still in a bank, which can induce an accelerated stall or a spin.

In General Aviation aircraft, full aileron deflection is highly effective at returning the aircraft to the horizon, while the rudder is utilized aggressively to arrest yaw and prevent a spin from developing.

(For theoretical context: If you progress to fly swept-wing transport category aircraft, these aerodynamics shift. Swept wings are highly susceptible to roll-coupling, and large underslung jet engines create massive pitching moments when power is added or reduced. However, the golden rule of aerodynamics remains universal regardless of the aircraft: you must always reduce the Angle of Attack first to regain control effectiveness before attempting to fix your roll or flight path.)

## Part 5: IFR Flight

### What Is Expected
Precision flying strictly by reference to instruments. You will master radial tracking, intercepts, and holding patterns, DME Arcs and approaches in the simulator as well as executing them in the air. Instrument scanning relies on the fundamental relationship: Power + Attitude = Performance.

To succeed in this environment, you must adopt the IFR golden rules:
*   act quickly and smooth,
*   firmly believe your instruments over your physical sensations to prevent sensory illusions (such as "the leans"), and
*   rely on the primary control mantra of Change - Check - Hold - Trim.

### IFR Threat & Error Management (TEM) and Flight Outlines
Managing disorientation and system failures is a major part of IFR operations. You must be prepared to fly on a limited panel if key instruments fail (for instance, covering the Attitude Indicator and Directional Gyro to simulate a vacuum failure).

To manage the intense single-pilot workload, mentally break down the IFR flight into its key phases. Every flight transitions through distinct phases:
*   Departure (SIDs or non-standard routing),
*   En-route (Airways, point-to-point tracking), and
*   Arrival (STARs, radar vectors, and stabilized approaches).

### SPISIM Sessions
You have 10 two hour sessions to cover the basics of Instrumental precision flying. You should focus on developing an instrument scan: Starting with the attitude indicator and short deviations to Airpseed Indicator / back to Attitude / Altitude / Attitude / Directional / Atittude / Vertical Speed.. Your eyes should constantly radiate out from the Attitude Indicator to the performance instruments (Airspeed, Altitude, Directional, Vertical Speed) and immediately back to the center.

The quicker you can develop this scan the better you will be at identifying parameters deviating from their intended values. It's all about catching the speed, altitude or heading as soon as it goes off your desired values and making corrective inputs to get them back. It's a constant adjustment and a intensive task.

To help you develop this scan you will find the B-Pattern and S-Turns in the IFR SOP. Your instructor will variations of these patterns and give you instructions simulating timed standard rate turns, climbs and descends in different configurations and speeds. It might seem boring but it is the perfect gateway to fly in the simulator (where you have no sensations of movement as well as no visual cues) and flying multi engine, complex aircraft where small deviations develop very quickly into unstable approaches.

Keep in mind that tolerances under IFR are incredible tight and workload, especially in single pilot operations is very intense. The SPISIM course is your best preparation for this. There is some flexibility what can be covered but at the very least you should be confidentl and quickly be able to intercept radials, fly different configurations and speeds without noticeable deviations during various phases of flight and be able to maintain altitudes, turn, climb and descend with various rates, speeds and configurations without deviations from rate one turns or other parameters. To test this you should be able to perform B-Pattern and S-Turns without prompts, errors and help from the Instructor.

### Continuous Descent Final Approach (CDFA) & Stabilized Approach
Modern instrument flying utilizes CDFA profiles rather than step-down dive-and-drive techniques. Apply an operational safety buffer (converting MDA to DA) so that any momentary sink during a missed approach never breaches published minimums. Enforce strict stabilized approach criteria: gear down, configured, speed stable, and sink rate controlled. Deviations require an immediate go-around.

### Mental Math & Descent Rules of Thumb
When flying a CDFA, stay ahead of the aircraft by continuously cross-checking your path using these simple calculations:
*   **Target Rate of Descent (R.O.D.):** To find the required descent rate for a standard $3^{\circ}$ glideslope, multiply your Groundspeed by 5. Alternatively, you can divide your Groundspeed by 2 and multiply by 10.
    *   *Analog Cockpit Tip:* If you do not have a GS display, use your current Indicated Airspeed (IAS) to do the math, then add or subtract a few feet per minute based on your estimated headwind or tailwind.
*   **Distance-to-Altitude Check (The 300-Foot Rule):** On a $3^{\circ}$ glidepath, the aircraft should lose 300 feet of altitude for every 1 NM flown. Use this to verify your descent profile against your DME (e.g., at 3 NM from the threshold, you should be approximately 900 feet AGL).
*   **Level-Off Anticipation:** Whether leveling off at an MDA or a step-down fix, prevent descending below your target altitude by anticipating the level-off by 10% of your current Rate of Descent (R.O.D.). If you are descending at 500 fpm, initiate the level-off 50 feet early.

### Point to Point Navigation
To navigate point to point, you will need the radial and distance where you are (Currently) and the point (radial and distance) you want to fly to (Desired), this is in reference to the same NAV Aid.
*   For example Current: R360 20 NM // Desired: R090 10 NM

**Cockpit Principle: RMI / CDI Compass Card**
*   Center of instrument: NAVAID facility.
*   Outer rim: Scaled to the greater of the two DME distances.
*   Inner radius: Scaled proportionally to the shorter distance.
*   Vector direction: Always from Current Position to Desired Position.

**Step-by-Step Procedure**
1. **Establish Scale**
   *   Compare Current DME vs. Desired DME.
   *   Let the outer rim of the compass card equal the larger distance.
   *   Determine the proportional radius for the smaller distance (e.g., 10NM is half-way out if the outer rim is 20NM, 5NM would be 1/4th of the scale.
2. **Plot the Two Fixes**
   *   Current Position: Locate your current radial on the card at its scaled DME distance. (If using an RMI, this lies along the tail of the bearing pointer).
   *   Desired Waypoint: Locate the target radial on the card at its scaled DME distance.
3. **Form the Vector**
   *   Visualize a straight line connecting Current Position to the Desired Position.
   *   Mentally slide (parallel) that line through the center of the instrument to read the magnetic track on the compass rose.
4. **Turn and Update**
   *   Turn directly to that track (applying an estimated wind correction angle).
   *   Recheck the vector periodically en route to refine your track as the DME and radial change.
